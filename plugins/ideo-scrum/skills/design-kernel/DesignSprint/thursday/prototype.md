# Prototype

> Source: Knapp, J., Zeratsky, J., & Kowitz, B. (2016). *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days.* Simon & Schuster. Chapter: "Fake It."

In old Western movies, the town looks real—but the saloon is just a façade, an exterior wall with nothing behind it. The audience doesn't care. They get lost in the story.

Thursday is about illusion. Instead of building your solution over weeks or months, you fake it. A façade gets you to 90 percent real on day one. The storyboard provides the blueprint; the sketches supply the details.

The longer you spend, the more attached you become. After one day, you're receptive to feedback. After three months, you're committed. Your prototype doesn't need plumbing or wiring. Just a façade.

## The prototype mindset

Building a façade may be uncomfortable. To prototype your solution, you'll need a temporary change of philosophy: from perfect to just enough, from long-term quality to temporary simulation. We call this philosophy the "prototype mindset," and it's made up of four simple principles.

### 1. You can prototype anything

If you go into Thursday with optimism and a conviction that there is some way to prototype and test your product, you will find a way. You may have to be resourceful and invent your own methods. But if you stay optimistic and adopt the prototype mindset, there is almost always a way.

### 2. Prototypes are disposable

Don't prototype anything you aren't willing to throw away. Remember: this solution might not work. Don't give in to the temptation of spending a few days or weeks getting your prototype ready. You'll have diminishing returns on that extra work, and all the while, you'll be falling deeper in love with a solution that could turn out to be a loser.

### 3. Build just enough to learn, but not more

The prototype is meant to answer questions, so keep it focused. You don't need a fully functional product—you just need a real-looking façade to which customers can react.

### 4. The prototype must appear real

To get trustworthy results in Friday's test, you can't ask your customers to use their imaginations. You've got to show them something realistic. If you do, their reactions will be genuine.

How real is real enough? Show them something flimsy—a "paper prototype" made up of drawings, or a simplified wireframe—and the illusion will break. Once the illusion is broken, customers switch into feedback mode. They'll try to be helpful and think up suggestions. In Friday's test, customer reactions are solid gold, but their feedback is worth pennies on the dollar.

## Goldilocks quality

As our partner Daniel Burka says, the ideal prototype should be "Goldilocks quality." If the quality is too low, people won't believe the prototype is a real product. If the quality is too high, you'll be working all night and you won't finish. You need Goldilocks quality. Not too high, not too low, but just right.
