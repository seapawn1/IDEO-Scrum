# Lightning Demos

> Source: Knapp, J., Zeratsky, J., & Kowitz, B. (2016). *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days.* Simon & Schuster. Chapter: "Remix and Improve."

In 1908, a German woman named Melitta Bentz got fed up with gritty, bitter coffee. Back then, coffee was brewed by dunking a pouch of ground beans into boiling water—messy, inconsistent, and full of grit. Convinced there had to be a better way, Bentz went looking. She came across the blotting paper in her son's school notebook, designed for mopping up excess ink. It was thick, absorbent, and disposable. She punctured holes in a brass pot, placed the paper inside, added ground coffee and hot water—and invented the paper coffee filter. More than a hundred years later, it remains one of the best tools for brewing coffee.

We all want a flash of divine inspiration. But amazing ideas don't happen like that. The lesson of Melitta Bentz: **great innovation is built on existing ideas, repurposed with vision.** Coffee filters had been tried before (cloth). The blotting paper was just sitting there. In your sprint, you'll follow her example: **remix and improve—but never blindly copy.**

Tuesday morning is for gathering raw materials. The method is called Lightning Demos: take three-minute tours of great solutions—from other products, different domains, and within your own company. The best ideas almost never come from your own industry. Look for similar problems in different environments. Look for unfinished ideas inside your organization. If you search far afield and close to home, you're sure to uncover surprising and useful ideas.

## How to do

### 1. Make a list

Come up with a list of products or services to review for inspiring solutions. Think outside your industry or field, and consider inspiration from within your own company (or your own past work). In Flatiron's sprint, the team looked at products in the medical field, tools for filtering email, task apps that sorted to-dos, management software for projects and deadlines, airline flight notification configuration, and experimental projects their own engineers had built but not finished.

Everything you review should contain something good you can learn from. It's not helpful to review crummy products. Narrow down to your top one or two products per category. Write the collected list down.

### 2. Give three-minute demos

Go through each product on your list. For each one, spend about three minutes examining it and asking: **What's the big idea here that might be useful?**

If you're working with a team, the person who suggested each product gives the tour. If you're working solo, you are the tour guide—open each product, explore it, and capture what stands out.

### 3. Capture big ideas as you go

Don't rely on short-term memory. For each demo, make a quick note that captures:

- A simple headline naming the big idea
- A rough sketch or screenshot of the inspiring component
- The source (product name or URL)

For example, someone from the Flatiron team thought it would be interesting to see how comments worked in Google spreadsheets. They quickly demonstrated the software, wrote the big idea ("Inline Commenting"), and jotted a quick drawing. These notes are just to jog your memory later in the day, so they don't have to be fancy or detailed.

By the end of your Lightning Demos, you should have ten to twenty ideas captured. That's enough to make sure you've captured your best inspiration—but it's a small enough set that you won't be overwhelmed when you start to sketch. Most won't turn into anything, but one or two may inspire a great solution. If you look hard enough, you can usually find your blotting paper.

## The source material

When you combine the ideas you just captured with Monday's map, your sprint questions, and your How Might We notes, you've got a wealth of raw material. In the afternoon, you'll turn that raw material into solutions with the four-step sketch.
