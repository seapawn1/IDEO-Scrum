# Pick a target

> Source: Knapp, J., Zeratsky, J., & Kowitz, B. (2016). *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days.* Simon & Schuster. Chapter: "Target."

At the end of Monday, you've compiled the data — long-term goal, sprint questions, map, expert interviews, HMW notes. Like Marie Tharp mapping the ocean floor and discovering the Mid-Ocean Ridge, the most important part of your project should jump right out of the map.

Your final task on Monday is to choose a target: **who is the most important customer, and what's the critical moment of that customer's experience?** The rest of the sprint will flow from this decision. Throughout the week, you'll sketch solutions, make a plan, and build a prototype focused on that target moment.

Once you've clustered your team's How Might We notes, the decision is usually straightforward. It's the place on your map where you have the biggest opportunity to do something great — and also, perhaps, the greatest risk of failure.

## Examples

**Savioke** targeted the hotel guest (rather than hotel staff) and focused on the moment of delivery (rather than the elevator or lobby). If they got the delivery right, they could apply what they learned elsewhere.

**Blue Bottle Coffee** targeted their most challenging audience: customers who had never heard of their cafés and were shopping for beans they had never tasted. If they could convince strangers, the online store would work for fans too.

**Flatiron Health** targeted research coordinators — not doctors — at the moment of searching for trial matches. It was the top of the funnel, where the most patients could be evaluated, and coordinators would be more tolerant of an imperfect new system.

## How to decide

The Decider needs to choose one target customer and one target event on the map. Whatever she chooses becomes the focus of the rest of the sprint — the sketches, prototype, and test all flow from this decision. By Monday afternoon, after a full day of mapping, interviewing, and note-taking, the target should be clear. If you're working solo, you are the Decider — review your map and HMW clusters, and make the call.

Once you've selected a target, take a look back at your sprint questions. You usually can't answer all of them in one sprint, but one or more should line up with the target. For example, Flatiron Health's target — research coordinators searching for matching trials — matched their sprint question "Will clinics change their workflow?" By testing a solution with real coordinators, they hoped to learn the answer. The target and the sprint question lined up perfectly.

By Monday afternoon, you've identified a long-term goal and the questions to answer along the way. You've made a map and circled the target for your sprint. Next, on Tuesday, it's time to come up with solutions.
